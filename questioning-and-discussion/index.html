04Questioning & Discussion

The questions a teacher asks, and the talk they allow to follow, shape what thinking happens in a room.

Rooted in the work of Alexander, Rowe, Mercer and others

The Quality of Questions

01 🔍Questions That Make Thinking Visible

Anderson and Krathwohl map six levels: remembering, understanding, applying, analysing, evaluating, creating. These are not a hierarchy of worth. Recall is foundational, not inferior.

Suggested technology question bank or lesson planning tool · Informed by Anderson & Krathwohl

Find this under Ask better questions · Make thinking visible

02 📈Surface, Deep and Transfer

Hattie and Zierer distinguish surface (what), deep (why) and transfer questions. Each serves a different stage. Reaching transfer before surface is secure sets students up to fail.

Suggested technology discussion prompt or slide display tool · Informed by Hattie & Zierer

Find this under Ask better questions

03 ❓Open Questions Demand More

Dillon found genuine open questions, where the teacher does not know the answer, produce the most extended student reasoning. Not every question needs a predetermined destination.

Suggested technology shared whiteboard or discussion board · Informed by Dillon

04 💡Plan Questions Before the Lesson

Improvised questions default to recall, not because recall is wrong but because planning stops there. Anderson and Krathwohl works as a planning tool: write questions at every level.

Suggested technology lesson planning or slide preparation tool · Informed by Anderson & Krathwohl

Find this under Ask better questions

05 🗣Alternatives to Questions

Dillon found statements and silence elicit richer responses than questions. Try: Tell me more. I had not considered that. Not every probe needs to end with a question mark.

Suggested technology discussion or annotation tool · Informed by Dillon

Find this under Ask better questions

Wait Time & Thinking Space

06 ⏰Wait Time 1: After You Ask

Rowe found most teachers wait under one second before speaking. Extending to three seconds increases the length and complexity of responses. Silence is thinking, not a gap to fill.

Suggested technology timer or countdown display tool · Informed by Rowe

More on this strategy

07 🕐Wait Time 2: After a Student Speaks

Rowe identified a second wait time: the pause after a student responds. Three seconds prompts elaboration and self-correction. Silence after an answer signals that more is expected.

Suggested technology live polling or response capture tool · Informed by Rowe

Find this under Ask better questions · Inclusive practice: participation and response

08 📝Write Before You Speak

Ask students to write before any verbal response is taken. Writing prevents the quickest from foreclosing others’ thinking. The pen democratises the room before the voice takes over.

Suggested technology shared doc, whiteboard or response tool · Informed by Tobin; Rowe

09 👥Think-Pair-Share

Lyman’s think-pair-share gives every student thinking time before public exposure. Pairs test ideas with a peer first. The result is more considered and more substantive discussion.

Participation & Equity

10 ✋Hands Down

Wiliam shows hand-raising distorts who answers. A small group volunteers repeatedly; the rest disengage. Remove hands and direct to named students. Every student should be asked.

Suggested technology random name picker or class list tool · Informed by Wiliam

More on this strategy

11 🎲Cold Call Without Threat

Good and Brophy showed differential questioning reinforces attainment gaps. Direct questions are equitable only when the climate supports it. Norms: no wrong attempts, only thinking.

Suggested technology random selector or seating plan tool · Informed by Good & Brophy; Wiliam

Find this under Increase participation · Inclusive practice: access and participation

12 📊Track Who You Ask

Good and Brophy found teachers direct harder questions to higher-attaining students. Track participation across lessons. Patterns invisible in the moment become visible on paper.

Suggested technology participation tracker or tally tool · Informed by Good & Brophy

Find this under Increase participation · Inclusive practice: participation and response

13 🎯Pose, Pause, Pounce, Bounce

Wiliam’s PPPB: pose the question, pause, direct to one student, then bounce the answer to another. The bounce keeps all students alert and spreads thinking across the whole room.

Suggested technology display timer; random name tool · Informed by Wiliam

Dialogic Teaching & Discussion

14 💬Five Principles of Dialogic Talk

Alexander identifies five qualities of dialogic talk: collective, reciprocal, supportive, cumulative and purposeful. Most talk meets two or three of these. Audit and start with the gaps.

Suggested technology lesson observation or reflection tool · Informed by Alexander

Find this under Improve classroom discussion

15 ▶️Break the IRE Pattern

IRE (Initiation, Response, Evaluation): ask, answer, judge. Alexander shows this pattern limits thinking. Replace evaluation with a follow-up question. What makes you say that?

Suggested technology video reflection or lesson capture tool · Informed by Alexander; Mercer

Find this under Improve classroom discussion

16 🗣Exploratory Talk

Mercer distinguishes disputational, cumulative and exploratory talk. Only exploratory talk, where students challenge ideas with evidence and build on each thinking, improves outcomes.

Suggested technology collaborative doc or discussion tool · Informed by Mercer

Find this under Improve classroom discussion

17 📚Ground Rules for Talk

Mercer found teaching norms of exploratory talk transforms discussion. Norms co-constructed with the class outperform those displayed or modelled. Make the ground rules together.

Suggested technology shared doc or display tool · Informed by Mercer

Find this under Improve classroom discussion

18 👨‍🏫Accountable Talk

Michaels, O’Connor and Resnick hold students to accurate knowledge and rigorous reasoning. Teach moves: I agree because..., The evidence is..., Say more.... Language shapes thinking.

Suggested technology discussion sentence starter display · Informed by Michaels, O’Connor & Resnick

Find this under Improve classroom discussion · Inclusive practice: language and explanation

19 🔁Build on Student Responses

Alexander’s EEF programme showed gains when teachers extended rather than evaluated. Return an answer: Aisha says X. Does anyone want to add or challenge? Conductor, not judge.

Suggested technology live display or response capture tool · Informed by Alexander

Student-Generated Questions

20 ✍️Teach Question Stems

King found students trained to generate questions using stems produce richer discussion than those answering only teacher questions. Generating questions demands deeper processing.

Suggested technology question stem cards or display tool · Informed by King

Find this under Ask better questions

21 👥Reciprocal Peer Questioning

King’s reciprocal questioning has students generate and pose questions to each other, with neither knowing the answer. This produces genuine enquiry rather than rehearsal. Aim to think.

Suggested technology pair chat or response tool · Informed by King

22 🔎Questions as Evidence of Understanding

A student’s question reveals the depth of their understanding. Ask for one written question before leaving. Why does X cause Y? shows far more than What is X?. Questions are windows.

Suggested technology exit ticket or response tool · Informed by King; Wiliam

Find this under Make thinking visible

23 💬Interthinking Through Questions

Mercer’s interthinking describes shared cognition built through language. Student questions sustain joint enquiry. The goal is thinking in common, not performance for the teacher.

Suggested technology collaborative discussion or writing tool · Informed by Mercer

Find this under Improve classroom discussion

24 📄Questions That Drive Enquiry

King found connecting questions to prior knowledge produces the most powerful learning. Ask students to write what they know and want to know. Their questions become the entry point.

The thinking

A classroom runs on its questions. Change the questions and you change the thinking.

Robin Alexander’s work on dialogic teaching showed us that talk is not the decoration around learning, it is often the medium of it. The problem is that classroom talk can easily become too thin, too quick, and too dependent on the teacher.

Neil Mercer’s work on exploratory talk helps here, because it shows what better classroom discussion looks like when pupils reason together, build on each other’s ideas and make their thinking public. Mary Budd Rowe’s work on wait time adds another important reminder: a few seconds of silence after a question can change the quality of what follows.

The strategies in this section move from better questions to better talk to the routines that help every voice count.

Silence after a question is not dead air; it is where better thinking often begins.

In practice

Technology can widen who gets to speak, but the thinking still has to belong to the student.

Anonymous response tools, shared documents and structured digital discussion can bring in students who might never raise a hand. That matters. They also give the class something to come back to, so a useful conversation does not disappear the moment the lesson moves on.

The risk, especially with AI, is that the tool answers the question students should be wrestling with. A good question slows thinking down. It gives students something to try, explain, challenge, refine and improve. When the tool removes that struggle, it can remove the learning too.

Use technology to bring more voices into the room, not to take the thinking out of it.

Use this for professional learning

A 30 minute activity for your team

Who does the thinking when you ask a question?. A 30 minute activity for a department, phase or professional learning group, using this guide as it stands.

  1. Read the thinking section above together.
  2. In pairs, recall this week's lessons: who answered, who never did, and how long the silence lasted before someone spoke.
  3. Explore the strategies and pick two or three that would spread the thinking wider.
  4. Discuss the mechanism: what changes when every student must compose an answer before any student gives one?
  5. Consider context: seminar-sized sixth form groups, classes of 32, students anxious about speaking.
  6. Ask whether technology helps, anonymous response perhaps, or whether hands-down and wait time cost nothing.
  7. Choose one strategy to trial for a week in one class.
  8. Agree what you'll count: who participates, and the length and quality of answers.
