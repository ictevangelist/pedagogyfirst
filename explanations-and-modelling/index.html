Chapter Five

05Explanations & Modelling

Telling is not explaining, and showing is not modelling. This chapter is about helping teaching work framed around how attention and memory actually work.

Rooted in the work of Rosenshine, Sweller, Mayer and others

Structuring Explanations

01 📚Small Steps, Always

Rosenshine: present new material in small steps with practice after each. Use animations to chunk information and reveal content gradually. Let students process before continuing.

Suggested technology slide animation; progressive reveal · Informed by Rosenshine

02 📦Concrete Before Abstract

Willingham: anchor new ideas to concrete examples first. Use images, video or objects to make the abstract visible before naming it. Move to the abstract once the concrete is secure.

Suggested technology visualiser; image or video clip · Informed by Willingham

03 🔗Connect to Prior Knowledge

Rosenshine: connect to prior knowledge before introducing anything new. Use a retrieval starter or concept map on screen to activate what students know. Link before you launch.

Suggested technology concept map or retrieval tool · Informed by Rosenshine

Find this under Explain difficult ideas · Help pupils connect prior and new learning

04 💡Clarity Over Novelty

The EEF found clarity matters more than delivery method. A diagram under a visualiser can outperform a polished video. Choose the tool that makes the idea clearest, not the newest.

Suggested technology any display tool used with purpose · Informed by EEF

05 ✅Check, Don’t Assume

Listening is not learning. Use live polls or mini whiteboards to check understanding at every step. Rosenshine: reteach before moving on. The explanation is the start, not the finish.

Suggested technology live polling or mini whiteboard tool · Informed by Rosenshine

Find this under Check understanding

06 ➕Supplement, Never Substitute

The EEF: technology works best when it supplements teaching, not replaces it. A screencast works alongside classroom explanation, not instead of it. Supplement, never substitute.

Managing Cognitive Load

07 🧠Four Slots, No More

Sweller: working memory holds about four items. Every screen element competes for those slots. Strip slides to essentials. Press B to blank the screen when you want attention on you.

Suggested technology B/W key; clean slide design · Informed by Sweller

Find this under Explain difficult ideas · Reduce unnecessary cognitive demand · Inclusive practice: working memory and cognitive demand

08 👀Avoid Split Attention

Sweller’s split attention: separate text and diagrams force learners to search between them. Integrate labels directly into the image. Use annotation tools to build the diagram live.

Suggested technology annotation tool; integrated diagrams · Informed by Sweller

Find this under Reduce unnecessary cognitive demand · Inclusive practice: working memory and cognitive demand

09 🔇Say It or Show It, Not Both

Sweller’s redundancy effect: narrating text on screen forces double processing. Say it or show it, not both. Use the B or W key to blank the slide while you talk through the idea.

Suggested technology B/W key in PowerPoint or Keynote · Informed by Sweller

Find this under Reduce unnecessary cognitive demand · Try this tomorrow

10 ❌Remove the Clutter

Mayer’s coherence: remove every word, image and sound that does not support learning. Strip clip art from slides. Cut background music. Every element on screen must earn its place.

Suggested technology clean slide template; minimal design · Informed by Mayer

Find this under Reduce unnecessary cognitive demand · Inclusive practice: working memory and cognitive demand

11 ➡️Signal What Matters

Mayer’s signalling: bold, arrows and highlights direct attention to what matters. Use annotation tools to signal live during the explanation. Without it, learners search alone.

Suggested technology annotation; slide highlight features · Informed by Mayer

Find this under Reduce unnecessary cognitive demand

12 ✂️Chunk and Reveal

Mayer’s segmenting: break content into chunks. Use slide animations to reveal one idea per click, not auto-play. Let working memory process before the next chunk arrives.

Multimedia & Dual Coding

13 🖼️Words and Images Together

Paivio’s dual coding: the brain processes words and images through separate channels. Design every slide with both: a key visual paired with minimal text. Two routes to the same idea.

Suggested technology dual coded slide design · Informed by Paivio

14 🎧Narrate, Don’t Label

Mayer’s modality: people learn better from images with narration than images with text. Record a narrated screencast over a diagram. Let the voice do what text would overload.

Suggested technology screen recording with voiceover · Informed by Mayer

15 📍Keep Words Near Images

Mayer’s spatial contiguity: place words near the images they describe. Labels sit beside the diagram, not in a separate list. Use slide design to keep related information together.

Suggested technology integrated slide layout · Informed by Mayer

16 🔄Progressive Reveal

Use animations to reveal content one step at a time. One idea per click, building the full picture gradually. Progressive reveal aligns the display to the pace of your explanation.

Suggested technology slide animation; appear on click · Informed by Mayer; Sweller

17 🎥Short Screencasts

Record short screencasts with narration over visuals. Mayer: images plus voice outperforms text-heavy slides. Keep each video to one idea. Brevity beats completeness every time.

Suggested technology screen recording tool; Loom or similar · Informed by Mayer

18 📖Pre-Train the Vocabulary

Mayer’s pre-training: teach key terms before the main explanation. A vocabulary slide or pre-lesson video builds familiarity first. Learners who know the words can focus on the ideas.

Modelling & Worked Examples

19 🗣Think Aloud

Rosenshine: model the process, not the product. Use a visualiser or screen share to think aloud live. Narrate your decisions as you work. Make invisible expertise visible to all.

Suggested technology visualiser; screen share with narration · Informed by Rosenshine

Find this under Make modelling clearer · Make thinking visible · Inclusive practice: language and explanation · Try this tomorrow

20 📷The Visualiser as Stage

A visualiser turns any desk into a modelling stage. Show handwriting, annotate text, build diagrams under the camera. The EEF found this among the most promising classroom technology.

Suggested technology visualiser or document camera · Informed by EEF; Rosenshine

Find this under Make modelling clearer

21 📝Worked Examples on Replay

Sweller and Cooper: students learn more from worked examples than equivalent problems. Record a screencast of the example so students can pause and study the method at their pace.

Suggested technology screen recording; LMS for sharing · Informed by Sweller & Cooper

Find this under Make modelling clearer

22 🔁Alternate Examples and Practice

Atkinson: alternate worked examples with practice. Display an example on screen, then set a parallel task. Example, try. Example, try. Interleaving builds understanding faster.

Suggested technology display and task alternating on screen · Informed by Atkinson; Sweller

Find this under Make modelling clearer

23 📉Fade the Scaffolds

Rosenshine: begin with full support, then fade. Show a complete example, then a partial one with gaps, then independent practice. Use fillable templates to structure the fading.

24 🌏Simulations Beyond Physical Limits

The EEF found simulations let students manipulate variables and see consequences beyond physical limits. Use to extend your explanation, not replace it. Technology serves pedagogy.

The thinking

Telling is not explaining. An explanation has to be built for the mind that receives it.

Telling is not explaining. We’ve learned through Rosenshine’s principles of instruction that distilled decades of research on effective teaching into a sequence that small steps, worked examples, checks for understanding, and scaffolds are removed as competence grows, helps.

Sweller’s cognitive load theory explained why this works, because working memory is narrow and easily overwhelmed, and good explanation manages that load rather than ignoring it.

Mayer’s multimedia principles then show how words and images should be combined, and how they should not.

I’ve integrated technology throughout the various categories, sharing ideas on how it can be used, when appropriate. Technology can often help, but it isn’t always the answer. You’ll always know your students better than anyone else, so keep them and your classes, front and centre in your thinking.

Working memory can overload. When explaining things with your class, it’s important to remember your role is often an act of load management.

In practice

Explanations and modelling are key to learning. Done well, the impact is significant, but it isn’t as simple as just showing content and talking about it...

Visualisers, screen recording and well-designed slides can help you model thinking step by step and replay it, which suits the way novices need to see a process unfold. Paivio laid it all out for us with dual coding theory, Sweller gave us cognitive load theory, and Mayer took these to give us the cognitive theory of multimedia learning. From this, we know to narrate a diagram rather than captioning it, cut the decorative extras, and never make the eye choose between competing sources at once.

The commonest mistake is the busy slide that feels rich but teaches little, because it forces split attention precisely when the explanation needs space. Design the explanation for how attention works. Pedagogy sets the rules; tech supports and enhances but doesn’t replace or offload.

Use technology to support explanation and modelling, never to replace or become the process.

Pedagogy informs the approach; technology enhances and supports.

Further reading and evidence

Where this thinking comes from

The wider evidence around this area, kept deliberately short. No single study validates all 24 strategies; these are the traditions the thinking comes from, and good places to read further. How this site uses evidence.

  • Rosenshine (2012), Principles of Instruction, American Educator. Ten principles, small steps and guided practice among them, drawn from cognitive science and studies of effective teachers.
  • Sweller, van Merriënboer & Paas (2019), Cognitive Architecture and Instructional Design: 20 Years Later, Educational Psychology Review. Cognitive load theory reviewed by its authors, including where the theory has been revised.
  • Mayer, Multimedia Learning (Cambridge University Press). The principles behind dual coding done properly: coherence, signalling, segmenting, modality and the rest.
  • Willingham, Why Don’t Students Like School?. Memory is the residue of thought, and why concrete examples and stories work.

Use this for professional learning

A 30 minute activity for your team

Bring a real explanation, and make it clearer. A 30 minute activity for a department, phase or professional learning group, using this guide as it stands.

  1. Read the thinking section above together.
  2. Each colleague brings one explanation or slide deck they'll teach next week.
  3. Explore the strategies, especially the cognitive load group, and audit the materials against them.
  4. Discuss the mechanism: what is competing for your students' working memory in each example?
  5. Strip one slide deck as a group: fewer words, integrated labels, one idea per reveal.
  6. Ask where technology genuinely helps, a visualiser or a short screencast, and where it adds noise.
  7. Each colleague commits to one revised explanation next week.
  8. Agree how you'll judge it: what students could do unaided afterwards, compared with the last topic.

The model behind this, and how to run it across a school or trust, is on the professional learning page.
