Reflection Feeds Teaching

The strategy, as published

Exactly as it appears on the card

15 💬Reflection Feeds Teaching

Butler: self-regulation and instruction are a loop. Use what students notice about their own errors to decide what you reteach, so their reflection shapes the next lesson.

Suggested technology exit poll of common errors · Informed by Butler

Part of Self-Reflection & Evaluation in 06 Metacognition & Self-Regulation, where it sits alongside its neighbouring strategies, the infographic and the thinking for the whole area.

Why you might use it

The problem it may help with

Reflection is usually treated as the pupil's business, so they notice their errors and they act on them. Butler's insight is that self-regulation and instruction are a loop, and the teacher is inside it, which means that what pupils notice about their own errors is evidence you can plan from.

It changes how pupils treat reflection too, because when Monday's lesson visibly begins from what they reported on Friday, reporting honestly starts to have a point.

What it might look like

One version of it, in a classroom

At the end of the lesson, an exit poll asks each pupil to name the error they made most, or the step they felt least sure of. The teacher tallies the responses, and the next lesson opens by reteaching the most common one, saying so out loud, along the lines of most of you said the same thing, so that's where we're starting.

Try it

A way to begin

Ask one exit question this week, such as "which part are you least sure of?", and then act on the tally where the class can see you doing it.

What to notice

Reading what happens

  • Whether what pupils report matches the errors actually in their work. When it doesn't, that mismatch is your next teaching point about self-monitoring.
  • Whether the self-diagnosis gets sharper once pupils see their reports being used, because it usually does when reporting honestly visibly leads somewhere.

Things to think about

Before and while you use it

  • The visible acting-on is what makes this work, because collected reflections that just vanish teach pupils that reflecting isn't worth their effort.
  • Where the reports and the work disagree, the reflection is still useful, just in a different way, because it tells you the monitoring needs teaching, and not only the content.

Related strategies

Where this connects

  • 🌧️ The Muddiest Point At lesson close, each student anonymously writes the one thing they are least clear about. The teacher addresses the most common muddy points publicly at the start of the next lesson. Two minutes; high value. 02 Formative Assessment · Students as Owners of Their Learning
  • 📋 Whole Class Feedback Read the set and identify patterns: common strengths, recurring errors, one priority next step. Teach to the gap, not to each paper. Sustainable for teachers; precise for students. 03 Feedback · Source & Direction
  • 🔀 Hinge Questions A hinge question is a diagnostic question at a lesson turning point. Wrong answers each map to a known misconception. Responses tell you whether to move on or go back. Never improvise it. 02 Formative Assessment · Elicit Evidence of Learning
  • 🔄 Turn Reflection Into a Plan Butler: feedback only regulates learning if it changes the next action. Have students convert each reflection into one concrete adjustment they will make on the very next task. 06 Metacognition & Self-Regulation · Self-Reflection & Evaluation

The wider thinking

Where the evidence sits

Butler frames instruction and self-regulation as a single loop rather than as parallel tracks, and this strategy is that loop run deliberately, with your next teaching move as its output. The card’s attribution stands as published: Butler.
