Break the IRE Pattern

The strategy, as published

Exactly as it appears on the card

15 ▶️Break the IRE Pattern

IRE (Initiation, Response, Evaluation): ask, answer, judge. Alexander shows this pattern limits thinking. Replace evaluation with a follow-up question. What makes you say that?

Suggested technology video reflection or lesson capture tool · Informed by Alexander; Mercer

Find this under Improve classroom discussion

Part of Dialogic Teaching & Discussion in 04 Questioning & Discussion, where it sits alongside its neighbouring strategies, the infographic and the thinking for the whole area.

Why you might use it

The problem it may help with

The IRE pattern of initiation, response and evaluation, or ask, answer and judge, is the default pattern of classroom talk, and Alexander shows that it limits thinking, because every exchange is three moves long and ends with a verdict from the front.

The fix on the card is small enough to try today. Replace the evaluation with a follow-up question, such as what makes you say that, so the exchange stays open and the reasoning has to come out.

What it could look like

One way in your classroom

A pupil answers that the metal expands when heated. Instead of saying "good", you ask "what makes you say that?" The pupil explains, and the explanation shows what they think the particles are doing, which turns out to be exactly the thing worth teaching next.

Try it

A way to begin

Pick one lesson and replace every evaluative response with either a follow-up question or three seconds of nothing at all.

What to notice

Reading what happens

  • How often you evaluate. The pattern is nigh on impossible to hear from the inside, which is why the card points at capturing a lesson to listen back to.
  • How long the exchanges become when the third move is a question rather than a verdict.
  • Whether pupils start justifying their answers before being asked to.

Things to think about

Before and while you use it

  • What makes you say that works on right answers too, because probing only the wrong ones teaches the class to read your follow-ups as a signal.
  • Wait time 2 breaks the same pattern with silence rather than a question, and some exchanges want one and some want the other.
  • Capturing a lesson to listen back to means recording people, and that needs consent. Whether and how you record is down to your school or trust policy, so seek that answer before you press record rather than after.

The wider thinking

Where the evidence sits

Alexander and Mercer both treat IRE as the pattern to escape from, and most of the dialogic cluster is, one way or another, an alternative third move. The card’s attribution stands as published: Alexander; Mercer.
