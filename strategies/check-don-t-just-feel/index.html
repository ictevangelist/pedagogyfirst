Check, Don't Just Feel

The strategy, as published

Exactly as it appears on the card

06 👀Check, Don't Just Feel

Winne: a novice's sense of understanding is unreliable. Pair every self-check with an external test, a quiz or worked answer, so monitoring rests on evidence rather than confidence.

Suggested technology self-marking quiz with feedback · Informed by Winne

Part of Performance & Monitoring in 06 Metacognition & Self-Regulation, where it sits alongside its neighbouring strategies, the infographic and the thinking for the whole area.

Why you might use it

The problem it may help with

Feeling that you understand something isn't evidence that you actually do. Winne showed that a novice's sense of understanding is unreliable, and it's least reliable exactly when it feels strongest, which is just after re-reading familiar material.

The fix is structural rather than motivational. Pair every self-check with an external test, such as a quiz or a worked answer, so that the monitoring rests on evidence rather than on confidence.

What it might look like

One version of it, in a classroom

At the end of a topic segment, pupils rate how secure they feel on it, then straight away take a five-question quiz. The two results sit side by side. For some pupils they'll agree, but for others the quiz disagrees with the feeling, and that disagreement is really the most useful thing the lesson produces.

Try it

A way to begin

Next time you hear yourself ask "are we happy with this?", follow it up with three quick questions and let the answers do the replying.

What to notice

Reading what happens

  • Pupils who were confident and wrong. They're the reason the external check exists in the first place.
  • Whether pupils start asking for the check themselves, because that's the habit starting to become their own.

Things to think about

Before and while you use it

  • Keep the checks low-stakes. The point is calibration rather than judgement, and stakes tend to distort both.
  • The check has to lead to something being done, because a gap that gets discovered and then ignored hasn't really helped anyone.

Related strategies

Where this connects

  • ❔ Ask Yourself As You Go Flavell: monitoring means questioning your understanding mid-task. Teach students to pause and ask “do I actually understand this?”, so gaps surface in time to fix them. 06 Metacognition & Self-Regulation · Performance & Monitoring
  • 📊 Compare Predicted to Actual Winne: calibration improves when students see the gap between expected and real scores. Have them predict a mark, then compare, so monitoring is corrected by evidence over time. 06 Metacognition & Self-Regulation · Self-Reflection & Evaluation
  • 🔬 Teach the Illusion of Knowing Show students their own illusion of knowing. Ask them to rate confidence before revealing the answer. When they were certain and wrong, that is the precise moment to intervene. 01 Retrieval Practice · Metacognition & Calibration
  • ✅ Provide Instant Feedback Metcalfe shows instant feedback is essential for confident errors. For general retrieval, allow a processing gap before revealing answers. Speed matters most when confidence is highest. 01 Retrieval Practice · Metacognition & Calibration

Technology second

Where technology fits

A self-marking quiz in your setting's approved tool gets the evidence back while the feeling of understanding is still fresh, and that's when the comparison teaches most. The important thing is to be clear about the division of labour, because the tool supplies the evidence, but the pupil still does the judging of what it means and what to do next, and that part isn't for automating.

The wider thinking

Where the evidence sits

Winne's model of self-regulated learning puts flawed self-monitoring right at its centre, because learners regulate on the basis of what they believe about their progress, so those beliefs need correcting with evidence. The card’s attribution stands as published: Winne.
