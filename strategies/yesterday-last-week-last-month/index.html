Yesterday, Last Week, Last Month

The strategy, as published

Exactly as it appears on the card

07 🔄Yesterday, Last Week, Last Month

Three questions per lesson: one from yesterday, one from last week, one from last month. These intervals are a starting point; complex topics may need revisiting more frequently.

Suggested technology quiz tool; lesson slide template · Informed by Rosenshine; Bjork

Find this under Help pupils remember more · Help pupils connect prior and new learning · Try this tomorrow

Part of Spacing & Interleaving in 01 Retrieval Practice, where it sits alongside its neighbouring strategies, the infographic and the thinking for the whole area.

Why you might use it

The problem it may help with

Spacing retrieval out over time is one of the most robust findings in memory research, and it's also one of the hardest to do consistently, because it needs the calendar as much as it needs the lesson plan.

This routine gets around the consistency problem by making the spacing automatic. You ask three questions per lesson, at three different distances, and older material keeps coming back around without you having to track anything at all.

What it could look like

One way in your classroom

Your starter slide carries the same three headings every lesson: yesterday, last week, last month. Writing tomorrow's slide is quick work with the medium term plan open, and pupils answer from memory before any books come out.

Try it

A way to begin

Add the three headings to your existing starter template and fill them in for one class tomorrow. Getting the routine going helps far more than agonising over the perfect choice of questions.

What to notice

Reading what happens

  • Which distance the class struggles with. If yesterday's question lands but last month's doesn't, that's really useful information, because it shows you exactly how far back the forgetting reaches.
  • Whether the same older topic keeps failing. The card is explicit that the intervals are a starting point, and complex topics may well need revisiting more often.

Things to think about

Before and while you use it

  • It only works if the older material genuinely comes back around, which makes this a planning habit as much as a lesson habit. The guide puts it well: the calendar is part of the pedagogy.
  • When content never returns after it's been taught, pupils decide it no longer matters. This routine works against that, but only if you protect it when time gets tight.

Technology second

Where technology fits

A low-stakes quiz tool, the approved one in your setting, takes the marking out of this entirely and keeps the routine sustainable, and a spaced repetition tool can handle the intervals for you too. What no tool should do is soften the retrieval itself, because the struggle needs to stay with the learner.

Drafting three questions at three distances, every single lesson, is exactly the kind of repetitive drafting AI is good at, through the approved tool in your setting, and a STAIR-shaped prompt can do the heavy lifting:

Swap in your own subject, class and topics, and expect to go back and forth a few times before it's right, because that's the I in STAIR and the iteration really is essential. You choose the questions, and the tool just drafts them.

The wider thinking

Where the evidence sits

The spacing effect runs all the way from Ebbinghaus through to Cepeda's work on the best gaps to leave, and Dunlosky's reviews rank distributed practice among the most effective techniques available. The card’s attribution stands as published: Rosenshine; Bjork.
