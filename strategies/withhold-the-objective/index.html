Withhold the Objective

The strategy, as published

Exactly as it appears on the card

04 🔮Withhold the Objective

Ask students to name what they learned at the end rather than copy an objective at the start. Generating the answer embeds it far more reliably than copying from the board.

Suggested technology polling tool; whole class response · Informed by Wiliam

Find this under Try this tomorrow

More on this strategy

Part of Clarify Learning Intentions in 02 Formative Assessment, where it sits alongside its neighbouring strategies, the infographic and the thinking for the whole area.

Why you might use it

The problem it may help with

Copying an objective from the board at the start of a lesson produces writing, not thinking. Asking pupils to name what they learned at the end demands both: they have to survey the lesson and generate the answer themselves.

It also hands you a small piece of evidence you cannot get any other way: whether what they think the lesson was about matches what you think you taught. The two are not always the same.

What it might look like

One version of it, in a classroom

At the end of a science lesson, pupils write one sentence: what was today actually about? The teacher reads a handful aloud. Half the class describe the activity rather than the idea behind it, which tells the teacher exactly what to sharpen next lesson.

Try it

A way to begin

Once this week, skip the copied objective and ask for the sentence at the end instead. Compare what they write with what you had planned on the board.

What to notice

Reading what happens

  • Answers that describe the task rather than the learning. The guide draws that line deliberately: finishing the task is not the same as achieving the learning.
  • How close the class's version is to yours. A wide gap is not a failed lesson; it is unusually honest feedback on the teaching.

Things to think about

Before and while you use it

  • Withholding the objective is not the same as hiding the point. Pupils still need clarity about what they are doing and why while they work; what changes is who articulates the learning, and when.
  • Some schools expect objectives visible in every lesson. How this routine sits with that expectation is a judgement for the teacher in the room. You know your class and your context better than any guide does.

The wider thinking

Where the evidence sits

The mechanism is the generation effect: Slamecka and Graf found that producing an answer embeds it more reliably than copying one, even when the produced answer needs correcting. The card’s attribution stands as published: Wiliam.

The fuller picture, sources included, is in further reading and evidence for Formative Assessment.
