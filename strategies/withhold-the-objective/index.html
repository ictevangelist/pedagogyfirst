Withhold the Objective

The strategy, as published

Exactly as it appears on the card

04 🔮Withhold the Objective

Ask students to name what they learned at the end rather than copy an objective at the start. Generating the answer embeds it far more reliably than copying from the board.

Suggested technology polling tool; whole class response · Informed by Wiliam

Find this under Try this tomorrow

Part of Clarify Learning Intentions in 02 Formative Assessment, where it sits alongside its neighbouring strategies, the infographic and the thinking for the whole area.

Why you might use it

The problem it may help with

Copying an objective from the board at the start of a lesson doesn't ask for much thinking. Asking pupils to name what they've learned at the end asks for quite a lot, because they have to look back over the lesson and come up with the answer for themselves.

It also gives you a small piece of evidence you can't really get any other way, which is whether what they think the lesson was about matches what you think you taught. The two aren't always the same, and it's useful to find out.

What it could look like

One way in your classroom

At the end of a science lesson, pupils write one sentence on what today was actually about, and you read a handful aloud. Half the class describe the activity rather than the idea behind it, which tells you exactly what to sharpen up next lesson.

Try it

A way to begin

Once this week, try skipping the copied objective and asking for the sentence at the end instead. Then compare what they write with what you'd planned on the board, and see how close the two are.

What to notice

Reading what happens

  • Answers that describe the task rather than the learning. The guide draws that line deliberately: finishing the task is not the same as achieving the learning.
  • How close the class's version is to yours. A wide gap doesn't mean the lesson failed, but it is honest feedback on the teaching, and it's worth having.

Things to think about

Before and while you use it

  • Withholding the objective isn't the same as hiding the point. Pupils still need to be clear about what they're doing and why while they work, and what changes is who puts the learning into words, and when.
  • Some schools expect objectives to be visible in every lesson, and how this routine sits with that expectation is your judgement to make. You know your class and your context better than any guide does.

Related strategies

Where this connects

  • 🎯 Separate Intention from Task The learning intention is what to learn, not what to do. Finishing the task is not the same as achieving the learning. Keep them distinct in your planning and in what you say to students at every stage of the lesson. 02 Formative Assessment · Clarify Learning Intentions
  • 🤝 Build Success Criteria Together Ask students what excellent looks like before they begin. Their language, not yours, becomes the standard. Criteria they articulate themselves land far more deeply than criteria handed to them. 02 Formative Assessment · Clarify Learning Intentions
  • ❓ The Generation Effect Slamecka and Graf found generating an answer, even a wrong one, before being told it produces stronger retention than passive exposure. The effort of generation drives encoding, not the accuracy. 01 Retrieval Practice · Desirable Difficulties
  • 🌧️ The Muddiest Point At lesson close, each student anonymously writes the one thing they are least clear about. The teacher addresses the most common muddy points publicly at the start of the next lesson. Two minutes; high value. 02 Formative Assessment · Students as Owners of Their Learning

The wider thinking

Where the evidence sits

The mechanism here is the generation effect. Slamecka and Graf found that producing an answer embeds it more reliably than copying one, even when the answer you produce needs correcting. The card’s attribution stands as published: Wiliam.

The fuller picture, sources included, is in further reading and evidence for Formative Assessment.
