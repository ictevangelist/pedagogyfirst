Plan Questions Before the Lesson

Exactly as it appears on the card

04 💡Plan Questions Before the Lesson

Improvised questions default to recall, not because recall is wrong but because planning stops there. Anderson and Krathwohl works as a planning tool: write questions at every level.

Suggested technology lesson planning or slide preparation tool · Informed by Anderson & Krathwohl

Find this under Ask better questions

Part of The Quality of Questions in 04 Questioning & Discussion, where it sits alongside its neighbouring strategies, the infographic and the thinking for the whole area.

Why you might use it

The problem it may help with

Questions asked on the spur of the moment tend to default to recall, and the card is precise about why: not because recall is wrong but because planning stops there. In the flow of a lesson, the question that comes to mind is simply the one that's easiest to form.

Writing your questions before the lesson, at every level of Anderson and Krathwohl's taxonomy, is what turns the framework from a poster on the wall into a genuinely useful planning tool.

What it might look like

One version of it, in a classroom

Planning a lesson on rivers, a geography teacher writes six questions into the slides in advance, from what is erosion right through to how would you defend this village, and why there. In the lesson the questions are simply ready and waiting, instead of needing to be invented in the middle of an explanation.

Try it

A way to begin

For one lesson this week, write three questions in advance at levels you know you wouldn't improvise, and put them somewhere you'll actually see them.

What to notice

Reading what happens

  • The difference in students' answers between your planned questions and your improvised ones.
  • Which of your planned questions you actually asked, because the ones that never got used tell you something about the shape of the lesson.

Things to think about

Before and while you use it

  • Plan the pivotal questions rather than a script. The point is to have the harder questions ready, not to close down where the discussion goes.
  • Improvised questions keep their place too, because responsiveness is part of the craft. Planning removes the ceiling, it doesn't remove the improvising.

Where this connects

  • 🔍 Questions That Make Thinking Visible Anderson and Krathwohl map six levels: remembering, understanding, applying, analysing, evaluating, creating. These are not a hierarchy of worth. Recall is foundational, not inferior. 04 Questioning & Discussion · The Quality of Questions
  • 📈 Surface, Deep and Transfer Hattie and Zierer distinguish surface (what), deep (why) and transfer questions. Each serves a different stage. Reaching transfer before surface is secure sets students up to fail. 04 Questioning & Discussion · The Quality of Questions
  • 🔀 Hinge Questions A hinge question is a diagnostic question at a lesson turning point. Wrong answers each map to a known misconception. Responses tell you whether to move on or go back. Never improvise it. 02 Formative Assessment · Elicit Evidence of Learning
  • ❓ Open Questions Demand More Dillon found genuine open questions, where the teacher does not know the answer, produce the most extended student reasoning. Not every question needs a predetermined destination. 04 Questioning & Discussion · The Quality of Questions

Technology second

Where technology fits

A lesson planning or slide tool keeps your planned questions where you'll actually use them. The planning is the strategy, and the tool simply stores it.

Drafting a ladder of questions across the levels is planning work AI can really help to speed up, through the approved tool in your setting, with the framework the card names built into the prompt:

The levels are a planning tool rather than a hierarchy of worth, and recall is foundational, not inferior. Draft with the tool, then choose and reorder for your class, and keep iterating until the ladder fits the lesson you're actually teaching.
