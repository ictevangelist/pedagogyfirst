03 ✍️Brain Dump

A blank page, two minutes, no notes: write everything you remember. The imperfect attempt is the point. What students cannot recall shows precisely where teaching needs to return.

Suggested technology shared doc or whiteboard tool · Informed by Roediger; Karpicke; Agarwal

Find this under Try this tomorrow

Part of The Testing Effect in 01 Retrieval Practice, where it sits alongside its neighbouring strategies, the infographic and the thinking for the whole area.

Why you might use it

The problem it may help with

Most revision defaults to putting information back in front of pupils, so they re-read, they highlight and they copy out notes. The research in this guide points the other way, because retrieval, getting the knowledge out, does more for long term memory than another pass over the page.

A brain dump is the simplest possible version of that. It needs no preparation, no question writing and no technology, and it gives you something a quiz can't, which is a picture of what pupils reach for when nothing prompts them, and the order it comes out in.

What it might look like

One version of it, in a classroom

Two lessons into a new topic, a class gets a blank page and two minutes to write everything they can remember about last lesson, with no notes and no talking. Afterwards, pupils open their books and check what they missed. The teacher asks for one thing that didn't come back, and the answers help to shape the start of the next lesson.

Try it

A way to begin

Try ending one lesson this week with it. Give pupils two minutes and a blank page to write everything they can remember from today, and keep it out of any book you mark, because the point is the retrieval rather than the record.

What to notice

Reading what happens

  • What most of the class couldn't recall. The card says it plainly: that is precisely where teaching needs to return.
  • Who writes steadily and who freezes at the blank page. Freezing tells you something useful too, and cued recall is a gentler step back for those pupils.
  • Whether what comes back is what you'd have predicted, because it often isn't.

Things to think about

Before and while you use it

  • There has to be something there to retrieve. If pupils have only met the material once, a brain dump ends up measuring the teaching more than the pupils.
  • The imperfect attempt is the whole point, so try to resist tidying it up into a marked task. As soon as it carries stakes, pupils play safe and you lose the honest picture.

Technology second

Where technology fits

Paper does this job perfectly well. Where the approved shared document or whiteboard tool in your setting can add something is the speed of reading, because you can see thirty attempts at once and skim them for the gaps without collecting in a single book. The retrieval effort is the important part though, not the admin around it.

The wider thinking

Where the evidence sits

This sits squarely in the testing effect tradition. Roediger and Karpicke showed that the act of retrieving is itself a learning event, not just a way of measuring one. The card’s attribution stands as published: Roediger; Karpicke; Agarwal.

The fuller picture, sources included, is in further reading and evidence for Retrieval Practice.
