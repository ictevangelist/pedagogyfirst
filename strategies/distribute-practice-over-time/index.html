Distribute Practice Over Time

The strategy, as published

Exactly as it appears on the card

06 📅Distribute Practice Over Time

Spread retrieval across days and weeks rather than cramming it into one session. Build review points into your medium-term plan. The calendar is part of the pedagogy.

Suggested technology spaced repetition tool · Informed by Cepeda et al.; Ebbinghaus

Find this under Help pupils remember more

Part of Spacing & Interleaving in 01 Retrieval Practice, where it sits alongside its neighbouring strategies, the infographic and the thinking for the whole area.

Why you might use it

The problem it may help with

Ebbinghaus mapped the forgetting curve well over a century ago, and Cepeda's work put numbers on the gaps that help most. The finding hasn't really changed in all that time. Retrieval spread across days and weeks does more for retention than the same amount of practice squeezed into one session.

The hard part isn't believing it, it's timetabling it, and that's why the card aims this at your medium-term plan rather than your lesson plan. As the guide puts it, the calendar is part of the pedagogy.

What it might look like

One version of it, in a classroom

Planning a six-week unit, a teacher pencils the review points in before the content, so week three revisits week one, week five revisits weeks two and three, and the following unit opens by retrieving this one. Nothing about any single lesson changes very much, but the shape of the term does.

Try it

A way to begin

Take the medium-term plan for your next unit and mark two points where earlier content will come back. Booking the slots is the habit worth building, and the questions themselves can always be written later.

What to notice

Reading what happens

  • What the first spaced review reveals. Material that seemed really secure at the time will have faded, and that's the forgetting curve doing what it does, not a failure of your teaching.
  • Whether the review points survive contact with a busy term. They tend to be the first thing squeezed out when time is tight, so it's worth protecting them deliberately.

Things to think about

Before and while you use it

  • Massed practice feels better in the room, for you and for pupils, so it helps to know in advance that the spaced version will look worse lesson by lesson while doing more across the term.

Technology second

Where technology fits

Spaced repetition tools exist for exactly this problem. They bring older material back at the right intervals automatically, which matches what the research says while saving you the headache of tracking it all by hand. If your setting has an approved one, let it carry the scheduling. The intervals are a job the tool can do, but making sure that what comes back is worth retrieving is still yours.

The wider thinking

Where the evidence sits

Cepeda and colleagues put numbers on the gaps that Ebbinghaus first mapped, and Dunlosky's reviews place distributed practice in the top tier of study techniques. The card’s attribution stands as published: Cepeda et al.; Ebbinghaus.

The fuller picture, sources included, is in further reading and evidence for Retrieval Practice.
