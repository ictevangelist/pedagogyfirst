Task, Not Person

The strategy, as published

Exactly as it appears on the card

16 🧠Task, Not Person

Feedback that threatens self-concept decreases performance. "This paragraph needs restructuring" works. "You need to try harder" does not. Direct feedback at the work, not the person.

Suggested technology annotation tool focused on work · Informed by Kluger & DeNisi

Find this under Make feedback more useful · Inclusive practice: feedback and next steps

Part of Feedback & the Learner in 03 Feedback, where it sits alongside its neighbouring strategies, the infographic and the thinking for the whole area.

Why you might use it

The problem it may help with

The fastest way to make feedback backfire is to aim it at the person. Kluger and DeNisi found that comments which threaten self-concept decrease performance, because once a pupil's attention is on themselves, it isn't on the work any more.

The card's two examples show the difference really clearly. This paragraph needs restructuring tells the pupil what to work on, and you need to try harder tells them nothing except how they've been judged.

What it might look like

One version of it, in a classroom

Rereading his comments before returning books, a teacher rewrites each one whose subject is the pupil until its subject is the work, so you have not explained this becomes this step needs the reason it works, and you must check your work becomes three of these answers fail the estimate test.

Try it

A way to begin

Scan your last set of comments for sentences whose subject is the pupil, and rewrite each one so the subject is a specific piece of the work.

What to notice

Reading what happens

  • Whether returned work produces defensiveness or redrafting, because the difference often follows where the comments were aimed.
  • Which pupils you most often address person-first, because it may well be exactly the ones for whom work-directed comments would help most.

Things to think about

Before and while you use it

  • Task-directed doesn't mean cold, and the warmth can live in the conversation while the written feedback stays on the work.

Where this connects

The wider thinking

Where the evidence sits

Kluger and DeNisi's analysis holds that the closer feedback moves to the self, the less useful it becomes, and Hattie and Timperley's levels tell the same story from a different dataset. The card’s attribution stands as published: Kluger & DeNisi.
