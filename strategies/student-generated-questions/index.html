Student Generated Questions

The strategy, as published

Exactly as it appears on the card

18 💡Student Generated Questions

Ask students to write questions that could appear in an assessment on this topic. Collate the best and use them for review. Generating questions demands higher order thinking than simply answering them.

Suggested technology shared doc or quiz builder tool · Informed by Black & Wiliam

Part of Students as Learning Resources in 02 Formative Assessment, where it sits alongside its neighbouring strategies, the infographic and the thinking for the whole area.

Why you might use it

The problem it may help with

Answering a question exercises what a pupil knows, but writing one that could plausibly appear in an assessment asks for a lot more. They have to survey the topic, judge which ideas are assessable, and know the material well enough to say what a fair question and a correct answer look like. The card puts it directly: generating questions demands higher order thinking than simply answering them.

The questions come back to you as evidence too. What pupils choose to ask, and what they avoid, maps the topic as they see it.

What it might look like

One version of it, in a classroom

Closing a unit on rivers, each pupil writes two possible assessment questions with answers. The teacher collates the strongest into next week's review quiz, and notices that almost nobody has written a question about deposition, which says something about where the class's confidence runs out.

Try it

A way to begin

End one topic by asking pupils to write two questions that could be on the test, with answers, and then use the best of them in your next review.

What to notice

Reading what happens

  • The level of the questions, whether they're pure recall or questions that need explanation and application to answer.
  • The topics nobody writes questions about, because avoidance is data too.

Things to think about

Before and while you use it

  • A vague or unanswerable question usually points at a gap in understanding the content rather than the task, which makes it worth following up rather than discarding.

Where this connects

  • 👥 Reciprocal Peer Questioning King’s reciprocal questioning has students generate and pose questions to each other, with neither knowing the answer. This produces genuine enquiry rather than rehearsal. Aim to think. 04 Questioning & Discussion · Student-Generated Questions
  • ❓ The Generation Effect Slamecka and Graf found generating an answer, even a wrong one, before being told it produces stronger retention than passive exposure. The effort of generation drives encoding, not the accuracy. 01 Retrieval Practice · Desirable Difficulties
  • 🔎 Questions as Evidence of Understanding A student’s question reveals the depth of their understanding. Ask for one written question before leaving. Why does X cause Y? shows far more than What is X?. Questions are windows. 04 Questioning & Discussion · Student-Generated Questions

The wider thinking

Where the evidence sits

Black and Wiliam place this kind of activity with students as resources for, and owners of, their own learning: the assessment thinking starts to move from teacher to pupil. The card’s attribution stands as published: Black & Wiliam.
