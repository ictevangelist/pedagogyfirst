Concrete Before Abstract

The strategy, as published

Exactly as it appears on the card

02 📦Concrete Before Abstract

Willingham: anchor new ideas to concrete examples first. Use images, video or objects to make the abstract visible before naming it. Move to the abstract once the concrete is secure.

Suggested technology visualiser; image or video clip · Informed by Willingham

Find this under Explain difficult ideas · Support access and participation · Inclusive practice: language and explanation

Part of Structuring Explanations in 05 Explanations & Modelling, where it sits alongside its neighbouring strategies, the infographic and the thinking for the whole area.

Why you might use it

The problem it may help with

Abstract ideas are really hard to hold on to when there's nothing to attach them to, and Willingham's advice is to anchor new ideas to concrete examples first, so you make the thing visible and then you name it.

When you name the idea first, the load goes the wrong way round, because pupils are being asked to hold a definition in mind while they're still hunting around for what it actually refers to.

What it could look like

One way in your classroom

Before the word erosion appears anywhere, you play a short clip of a riverbank collapsing, and then show photographs of the same stretch of river a year apart. Only once the class has described what they can see does the term arrive to label it.

Try it

A way to begin

For one abstract term you'll be teaching next week, find the image, clip or object first, and plan to show it before you ever say the word.

What to notice

Reading what happens

  • Whether pupils reach for the concrete example when they're explaining the idea back to you.
  • What happens when the abstract term is tested later on, and whether the anchor comes back with it.

Things to think about

Before and while you use it

  • The concrete example is there to get pupils to the abstract idea, not to stay forever. The card is explicit that you move to the abstract once the concrete is secure, and judging when it's secure is a call only you can make, because you're the one in the room.

The wider thinking

Where the evidence sits

Willingham's cognitive science writing for teachers is the source here, and his point is that understanding is built out of things we already understand, which is why the concrete example comes first. The card’s attribution stands as published: Willingham.

The fuller picture, sources included, is in further reading and evidence for Explanations & Modelling.
