Test Before You Teach

The strategy, as published

Exactly as it appears on the card

04 📈Test Before You Teach

Quiz students before teaching. Struggling to retrieve primes the brain; students who fail before instruction remember the answer more reliably once taught. Failure is productive.

Suggested technology pre-lesson quiz tool · Informed by Bjork; Kapur

Part of The Testing Effect in 01 Retrieval Practice, where it sits alongside its neighbouring strategies, the infographic and the thinking for the whole area.

Why you might use it

The problem it may help with

Quizzing pupils on content they have not yet been taught sounds backwards, and that is exactly the card's claim: struggling to retrieve primes the brain, and students who fail before instruction remember the answer more reliably once taught.

It reframes failure as preparation. The wrong answers are not a problem to manage; they are the priming.

What it might look like

One version of it, in a classroom

Before a lesson introducing the causes of the First World War, a history teacher poses three questions the class cannot yet answer well and asks for best guesses. No marks, no discussion beyond a brief reveal. The lesson then teaches the material, and the earlier guesses give the new content something to attach to.

Try it

A way to begin

Open one new topic with two or three questions pupils cannot yet answer, asked for best guesses, before any teaching. Keep it brief; the priming is the point, not the score.

What to notice

Reading what happens

  • How pupils handle being asked what they do not know. The activity only works if guessing wrong is visibly safe.
  • Whether the pre-tested material comes back more reliably later than material introduced without the quiz.

Things to think about

Before and while you use it

  • Be explicit with the class about why you are doing it. Pupils told that failure here is productive respond differently from pupils who suspect they are being caught out.
  • This pairs naturally with the generation effect: a wrong answer generated before the right one is revealed is doing encoding work.

Where this connects

  • ❓ The Generation Effect Slamecka and Graf found generating an answer, even a wrong one, before being told it produces stronger retention than passive exposure. The effort of generation drives encoding, not the accuracy. 01 Retrieval Practice · Desirable Difficulties
  • 🧔 Embrace the Struggle Bjork coined desirable difficulties for conditions that slow apparent learning but improve long-term retention. Effortful retrieval is the clearest example. Resist the urge to make it easier. 01 Retrieval Practice · Desirable Difficulties

The wider thinking

Where the evidence sits

Bjork's work on pretesting and Kapur's on productive failure point the same way: unsuccessful retrieval before instruction can prepare memory for what follows. The card’s attribution stands as published: Bjork; Kapur.

The fuller picture, sources included, is in further reading and evidence for Retrieval Practice.
