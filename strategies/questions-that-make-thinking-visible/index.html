Questions That Make Thinking Visible

The strategy, as published

Exactly as it appears on the card

01 🔍Questions That Make Thinking Visible

Anderson and Krathwohl map six levels: remembering, understanding, applying, analysing, evaluating, creating. These are not a hierarchy of worth. Recall is foundational, not inferior.

Suggested technology question bank or lesson planning tool · Informed by Anderson & Krathwohl

Find this under Ask better questions · Make thinking visible

Part of The Quality of Questions in 04 Questioning & Discussion, where it sits alongside its neighbouring strategies, the infographic and the thinking for the whole area.

Why you might use it

The problem it may help with

Anderson and Krathwohl give you a really useful map of what a question is asking a learner to do, whether that's remembering, understanding, applying, analysing, evaluating or creating. Without a map like this, questioning tends to settle into one register, and the talk in your classroom can end up much thinner than the thinking you're actually after.

The card's caution is just as important as the taxonomy itself, which is that these are not a hierarchy of worth. Recall is foundational, not inferior, and a lesson full of analysis questions built on insecure knowledge doesn't really help anybody.

What it might look like

One version of it, in a classroom

Imagine a history teacher looking back across a fortnight of questions on the causes of the First World War. Asking what year or which countries is remembering. Asking why the alliance system raised the stakes is analysing. Asking whether any single country was most responsible is evaluating. Nothing about the teaching has changed yet, but the teacher can now see which kinds of thinking are being asked for and which ones never get a look in.

Try it

A way to begin

Write down the questions you asked in one lesson, from memory or from your slides, and place each one against the six levels. The spread you find, or the lack of it, is the useful bit.

What to notice

Reading what happens

  • Which levels come up again and again across a week, and which never appear at all.
  • Whether your recall questions are doing their foundational work before you ask for analysis or evaluation.
  • What happens to the answers when you deliberately ask at a level you rarely use.

Things to think about

Before and while you use it

  • The taxonomy is a map rather than a ladder to be climbed in every lesson. Which level a class needs next is a judgement about their knowledge, and that judgement is yours to make.
  • It pairs really naturally with planning questions before the lesson, because the levels are most useful before the room fills up.

Related strategies

Where this connects

  • 💡 Plan Questions Before the Lesson Improvised questions default to recall, not because recall is wrong but because planning stops there. Anderson and Krathwohl works as a planning tool: write questions at every level. 04 Questioning & Discussion · The Quality of Questions
  • 📈 Surface, Deep and Transfer Hattie and Zierer distinguish surface (what), deep (why) and transfer questions. Each serves a different stage. Reaching transfer before surface is secure sets students up to fail. 04 Questioning & Discussion · The Quality of Questions
  • 🔀 Hinge Questions A hinge question is a diagnostic question at a lesson turning point. Wrong answers each map to a known misconception. Responses tell you whether to move on or go back. Never improvise it. 02 Formative Assessment · Elicit Evidence of Learning
  • 💬 Retrieval Beyond Facts Factual retrieval builds the schema; higher-order retrieval tests whether students can use it. Secure the foundations first, then push beyond them. Retrieval is not a pub quiz. 01 Retrieval Practice · Retrieval Formats

The wider thinking

Where the evidence sits

Anderson and Krathwohl's revision of the taxonomy is one of the most widely used frameworks for question design, and their insistence that recall is foundational, not inferior, is the part that gets forgotten most often. The card’s attribution stands as published: Anderson & Krathwohl.
